Skills Application

MIT · 2013–2014

A short description with visuals of the Skills Application Period, a central component of the learning experience for the MIT-Skoltech (SKT) graduate engineering program.

Overview

There is a quote from Theodore von Kármán that reads, Scientists discover the world that exists; engineers create the world that never was. I first saw this quote at MIT and it has stuck with me since. But it was the perfect way to capture the core of our program and engineering education in general — learning by doing.

As an Instructional Designer my role was to create the conditions in which learners could meaningfully practice what they had learned. This translated into prototyping, teaming, and then pitching the idea (rather than presenting it) as if they were selling a new product. All of these were real-world skills that the faculty I partnered with aimed to provide. And so they were my goals too.

The learning model for the MIT-SKT program was very different from the standard 15-16 week university model. Rather than complete 4 courses during that time, learners would focus on 2 course at a time. Over a standard 16-week semester, learners time would break into the following pattern:

  • 6 weeks for 2 courses and learning activities
  • 1 week for assessment
  • 1 week for application
  • 2 day weekend break
  • 6 weeks for 2 courses and learning activities
  • 1 week for assessment
  • 1 week for application

Each Skills Application Period (week) would follow the CDIO learning cycle.

  • Conceive — day 1
  • Design — days 2-3
  • Implement — days 4-6
  • Operate — days 6-7
  • Celebrate! — day 8

By the end of the semester learners not only applied gained knowledge in their area of study, but they did it in teams, they practiced business skills, and they used cross-domain knowledge from courses in sector experiences.

Skills Application Period 1

The first skills application period ran during the middle of fall 2013 in Moscow, Russia. My colleagues and I traveled to Skoltech and stayed the week to observe and document how it unfolded. It was nothing less than awesome. The students were highly engaged from the start of the design process, through crafting how they would pitch the concept, incorporate feedback, build a prototype, and then demonstrate its value and sell it to the audience. Impact

This was the first of its kind event with no precedent. Its impact was in the engagement from faculty, staff, and students. What is more, the prototypes students created and practiced pitching were exactly the kind of evidence we were seeking. The how they did it was something that we knew could always improve and sought to make stronger in iterations that followed. Nonetheless, this event set a solid foundation for the skills application periods to come.

To this day, I am both proud and honored to have been a part of this kind of curricular and activity building at MIT. I think we all felt something special from it. I personally love building and it was great to be able to use the diversity of my knowledge and skills to contribute to building activies like this.
