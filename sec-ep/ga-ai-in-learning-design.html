AI in Learning Design

GA · 2026

A description of how I have incorporated Artificial Intelligence (AI) into my learning design process. I use Anthropic Claude for workflows and documents, and Google Gemini Pro as a content and technical researcher. I do not use them, though, to write actual content, activities, assessments, and other aspects of a learning experience.

Overview

I believe the use of AI in learning design should not be done to replace human skills and imagination. When creating a learning experience for humans, it must be made by humans. Trained educators are best suited to applying learning theory, creating content engagement, designing situations for learner interaction, designing assessments, and ultimately integrating the components that make a learning experience. I do not apply AI or advise anyone else to use AI by directing it to autonomously create any part of a learning experience. Instead, my approach to using AI is to have it help me build materials based on the thinking work I have done. The outputs that I produce serve as the context, constraints, guidelines, and as roadmaps for what I aim to ultimately build.

My preferred AI model is Anthropic Claude — primarily the Opus model. I use this through the desktop application and within VS Code. Using the desktop app, my process is to create a single project for all learning design. I write and update instructions for this project and share files for context that include the following: rules and guidelines I need to follow, project workflows, project notes, and frameworks I like to use. I also add notes on the project and client that cover background information and instructional plans that I am working on. (I provide all of these in Markdown format.) I then use this project as a project management and design partner that can help me keep alignment, recall past decisions, identify inconsistencies, recall previous ideas and content, and act as a rapid prototyper for all kinds of materials.

My approach to AI in learning design is to leverage it to speed up the processes and workflows of creating documentation from my design outputs. It is not to do the thinking for me. There are however occasions when I need ideas for how something could be sequenced and what kinds of activities might work. When this happens, I'll ask AI to help give me some ideas, but ultimately, I decide what to do. So for me, AI is my partner in the learning design process I've done for 20+ years.

Discovery process

Every project begins with a needs analysis, and this typically involves interviewing stakeholders. These are done with decision-makers, technical experts, exemplary staff members, and/or potential learners. To help get a head start planning these interviews, I shared with Claude some of my notes and questions I like to ask interviewees. After some back-and-forth, we created a reusable question bank. Design process

As I communicate to stakeholders, there are three core documents: 1) the High-Level Design (HLD) slide deck, 2) the Discovery-Design-Development (DDD) slide deck, and 3) the Competency Map. Building these can take a few hours each. But AI can lessen that time with written design documentation. While not a 1:1 match, I created and use Markdown templates of slide decks, and an HTML template of the competency map that AI can use in conjunction with my design plans to create initial drafts. All outputs are starting points only.

Impact

Using AI to speed up the process of creating documentation has been the most impactful aspect of using AI in learning design for me. With each project, I look for opportunities to expand our partnership in ways that I feel comfortable with and can trust. But that will take more planning and learning on my part. So far though, the time AI has saved has allowed me more time for people, planning, thinking, and even rest. This has worked because its outputs are based on my thinking work that I document, structure, and share.

Integrating AI into my work and learning design is an ongoing and developing process for me. I am intentionally taking steps and iterating on those as I see it evolve and monitor how much I allow it to take on. At the same time, I am very careful to avoid cognitive offloading. Nonetheless, I have found AI quite helpful in my work and particularly in the following ways:

  • answering questions on process;
  • drafting initial materials;
  • generating simple visuals;
  • researching content outside of my expertise;
  • rapidly building folder/file structures for distribution; and
  • discussing learning activity ideas based on my notes.
